Discriminator

If Levodopa Was Withheld, Certainty Had to Be Withheld Too

On therapeutic caution versus diagnostic discrimination.

They did not have to give levodopa. But if they did not give levodopa, they had to leave the diagnosis open.

The levodopa decision is the point at which the case becomes easiest to misunderstand and hardest to excuse. It is easy to misunderstand because a defence can make the decision look clinically respectable. The patient was young. Early levodopa exposure in younger Parkinsonian patients can carry long-term complications. A cautious clinician may decide not to rush toward the strongest dopaminergic therapy.

But this case does not turn on the narrow therapeutic axis. It turns on the diagnostic consequence of that therapeutic decision.

In this presentation, levodopa was not merely medication. It was information. It was not merely a treatment that might relieve symptoms; it was a discriminator that could clarify what kind of disorder was present, how rigidity and gait related to dopaminergic responsiveness, and whether the severe foot-centred syndrome belonged inside the movement disorder rather than outside it.

To withhold levodopa was therefore not only to delay therapy. It was to delay knowledge.

The argument is not that levodopa absolutely had to be started immediately in July 2017. That is too blunt. The stronger argument is more precise: if levodopa was not started, diagnostic certainty had to be withheld too. The institution could choose therapeutic caution, but it could not safely combine therapeutic caution with administrative finality.

A safe discharge could have said: suspected young-onset Parkinsonism; dopaminergic therapy deferred for long-term reasons; because response to levodopa may carry diagnostic significance, the diagnosis remains provisional; foot-centred symptoms and dystonic gait require further review. That would have preserved the truth of the situation.

Instead, the record deferred levodopa but did not defer certainty. It withheld the discriminator but allowed the diagnosis to harden.

The phrase “we may regret that we did not start this earlier” matters because it exposes risk. Read only as treatment, it means the patient may later have benefited from earlier symptom relief. Read diagnostically, in this atypical foot-centred case, it means the team may regret delaying the intervention that could have clarified the case before the diagnosis hardened.

If the feet improved, that would matter. If gait or rigidity changed, that would matter. If pain responded, that would matter. The response could help decide whether the feet were dermatological, peripheral, central, dystonic, Parkinsonian, genetic, or overlapping.

So when levodopa was withheld, diagnostic inquiry remained open. That follows from the logic of the decision at the time. The team did not apply a material discriminator. Therefore the diagnosis had not been discriminated by that route.

If caution is high, certainty should be low.

In this case, caution was high and certainty was high. That is the contradiction.

If senior advice shaped the deferral, the decision becomes more institutional, not less. A policy-shaped decision to withhold the discriminator creates an institutional obligation to avoid finality. If the senior strategy was not to start levodopa yet, the corresponding governance strategy should have been not to finalise yet.

The Long Term Illness certification makes the point sharper. A provisional diagnosis inside clinical reasoning is one thing. A certified diagnosis used for statutory purposes is another. Once Parkinson’s Disease is certified, it leaves tentative thought and enters administrative reliance.

The question becomes: was it appropriate to certify Parkinson’s Disease while the principal dopaminergic discriminator had been withheld for strategic reasons and the initiating foot-centred problem remained unresolved?

That is not a medical curiosity. It is a governance question. It is also a record-accuracy question.

The final point is ethical as well as clinical. If levodopa was being deferred in a way that affected not only treatment but diagnostic knowledge, that should have been explained. Without that explanation, the patient could understand the decision only as therapeutic caution. He could not understand it as diagnostic non-testing.

That manufactured ignorance matters. The patient later protested that the feet had not been addressed without the technical architecture that would have made the protest impossible to neutralise.

Levodopa was the withheld drug, the withheld test, the withheld explanation, and the withheld limit on certainty.

The proper rule is simple: if levodopa was withheld, certainty had to be withheld too.

The record did the opposite.
