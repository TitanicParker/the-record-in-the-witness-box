DAT scan

Confirmation After Closure

On the DAT scan addendum as retrospective reinforcement rather than governing inquiry.

The DAT scan was evidence. But in the sequence, it was evidence after closure.

The DAT scan addendum is the kind of document that can easily be made to look decisive if it is read out of sequence. It records an abnormal scan. It appears to support a Parkinsonian diagnosis. Read quickly, it may seem to settle the matter: the team suspected Parkinson’s disease, the scan was abnormal, therefore the diagnosis was justified.

That is too simple.

The DAT scan must be read in time. The question is not whether the scan was abnormal. The question is what role the scan played in the sequence. Did it govern an open diagnostic decision, or did it retrospectively reinforce a frame that had already become administratively final?

By the time the addendum arrived, the diagnostic and administrative machinery had already moved. Parkinson’s Disease had already been written as the primary diagnosis. The Long Term Illness certification had already identified Parkinson’s Disease as the condition for which the patient was under care. The patient had already been placed into a Parkinson’s pathway.

The scan therefore did not operate as the point at which the institution decided whether finality was justified. It operated as confirmation after expectation.

That is why the language “as expected” matters. It tells the reader that the scan was being interpreted through an existing diagnostic expectation. The result was not described as a fork in the road. It was framed as confirmation of what the clinicians already thought.

That does not make the scan irrelevant. It means the scan cannot bear the weight later placed on it. It may support impaired dopaminergic transport or a Parkinsonian syndrome. It does not answer the feet. It does not explain severe foot-centred pain. It does not decide whether the foot posture was dystonic. It does not resolve the significance of brisk reflexes. It does not substitute for levodopa response. It does not document or answer the patient’s protest.

Most importantly, it does not travel backwards in time and make earlier finality properly sequenced.

A later supportive test can support a clinical suspicion. It cannot justify the premature conversion of that suspicion into administrative fact if the conversion happened before the test governed the decision. Evidence used after closure is not the same as evidence used before closure.

The ordering breach is simple: finality should follow inquiry. If the DAT scan was part of the inquiry, finality should have waited for it. If the scan was not necessary for finality, then it cannot later be used as though it justified finality. The institution cannot close before the scan and then treat the scan as the basis for closure.

Support is not sequence.

The scan was not nothing. But it was not enough. It could support the suspicion. It could not erase the incompleteness. It could not answer the feet, supply the missing levodopa response, cure the absence of movement-disorder referral, restore the omitted protest, or make statutory certification timely.

The DAT scan was evidence.

But in the sequence, it was evidence after closure.

And evidence after closure is not inquiry before closure.
