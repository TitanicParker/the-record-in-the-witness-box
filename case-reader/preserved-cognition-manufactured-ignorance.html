Asymmetry

Preserved Cognition, Manufactured Ignorance

On cognitive capacity, withheld diagnostic architecture, and the asymmetry between what the institution knew and what the patient could know.

The patient could have understood. He was not told enough to do so.

Preserved cognition is one of the most important facts in the discharge record because it removes a defence the system might otherwise have had. The patient was not recorded as confused, cognitively impaired, or unable to understand complexity. The opposite is true. He was treated as unusually capable: insightful, able to take information on, able to retain it, able to question appropriately, able to understand nuance.

In an ordinary clinical record, that might look like a compliment. In this case, it becomes an indictment.

Because the record says the patient could understand. The question is what he was actually allowed to understand.

The patient was told that levodopa was being delayed. He could understand that as therapeutic caution: we are protecting your future; starting too soon may create complications. What he was not told, on the available account, is that levodopa also had diagnostic significance. He was not told that the medication being deferred might clarify the relationship between Parkinsonism, dystonia, rigidity, gait, pain, and the feet.

A patient can consent to therapeutic caution without understanding diagnostic foreclosure. He can believe he is accepting a treatment strategy while remaining unaware that the same decision means the diagnostic sequence has not been completed.

That is manufactured ignorance. Not manufactured stupidity. Not incapacity. Ignorance.

A cognitively preserved patient can be made ignorant if the institution withholds the organising fact.

This matters most at the moment of protest. Before discharge, the patient objected that the feet had not been addressed. He knew the body had not been answered. He knew the pain and gait problem remained unresolved. But he did not know enough to say: you have withheld the principal dopaminergic discriminator while certifying the diagnosis. He could only say: what about my feet?

The system could survive that sentence. It might not have survived the technical version.

The institution held the architecture: uncertainty, withheld discriminator, atypicality, genetic possibility, unresolved feet, and specialist limitation. The patient held the pain: the unresolved body, the feet, the gait, and the ordinary-language protest.

The discharge summary then preserved the institution’s architecture and omitted the patient’s protest. It preserved cognition as a neurological feature, not as a procedural safeguard.

If a patient is cognitively capable, the standard of explanation rises. The clinician cannot treat complexity as beyond him. The record itself says it was not beyond him. Therefore he was entitled to be told the operative nuance: that the diagnosis was not merely being treated cautiously, but left diagnostically incomplete by the withholding of a discriminator.

The later analysis did not arise from confusion. It arose from cognition finally supplied with the missing facts.

A safe record would have said that the patient understood levodopa was being deferred for long-term reasons, but also that dopaminergic response had not yet been assessed; that the diagnosis remained provisional; that the foot-centred symptoms remained under neurological review; and that his concern about the feet was documented.

Instead, the patient appears to have been given therapeutic explanation without diagnostic architecture. His protest remained trapped in ordinary language. The record then closed without preserving it.

It is possible to inform a patient extensively and still leave him ignorant of the one fact that would have allowed meaningful objection.

The barrier was not the patient’s mind. The barrier was the system’s disclosure.
