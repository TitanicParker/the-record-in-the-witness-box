The Defence Has to Fight Its Own Documents

On institutional ambiguity, patient experience, and the absurdity of defending a record by contradicting the record.

If the documents are ambiguous, the ambiguity was created by the institution. If the documents are not ambiguous, they support the patient.

The defence has one tempting escape route. It can say the case was never truly closed. It can say the patient remained under neurology follow-up, that the feet were repeatedly discussed, that alternative possibilities were sometimes considered, that pain medication was adjusted, that further scans or tests were contemplated, and that the record therefore remained open.

That argument sounds plausible only until the documents are read carefully.

Continuing appointments are not the same as continuing inquiry. A patient can be reviewed for years inside a closed frame. A symptom can be documented for years without being allowed to govern. A contradiction can be preserved indefinitely without being resolved.

The institution did not keep the case open. It kept the contradiction alive while keeping the diagnosis closed.

The case is powerful because it is built from the institution’s own documentation. The patient does not need to invent the contradiction. The records supply it. The referral preserves diagnostic openness. The discharge summary declares diagnostic finality while recording atypicality, unresolved foot-centred symptoms, dystonic-looking gait, deferred dopaminergic discrimination, genetic possibility, and complexity. The statutory certification gives the label administrative force. Later records keep returning to the feet, but do not let the feet displace the governing frame.

That is not a patient imposing meaning on an empty record. It is a patient reading the institution’s own record back to it.

Any ambiguity in the documentation is not neutral. It was created by the institution. The institution chose the headings, diagnosis line, certification, qualifications, omissions, and descriptions of the feet. If the resulting record has two possible interpretations, the ambiguity belongs to the author of the record, not the patient who had to live inside it.

The patient experience becomes evidentially important because it is the continuity test. It asks which interpretation of the record matches what actually happened. On the patient’s account, the foot problem was not resolved at discharge. The later record confirms the same continuity: years later, the feet are still the recurring and central complaint, still linked to medication timing, still oscillating between Parkinson-related, not directly Parkinson-related, neuropathic, dystonic, peripheral, amplified, and medication-responsive.

That is not closure. It is unresolvedness under management.

The record cannot use Parkinson’s to absorb the feet when it needs finality, and then deny that pain is Parkinsonian when the patient says the feet were never explained. If pain is not really a direct part of Parkinson’s, then Parkinson’s did not resolve the foot-centred presenting problem. If Parkinson’s medication relieves the pain, then the foot problem is not incidental, merely podiatric, or safely outside neurology.

The defence may try to say the record was nuanced. But nuance is not a licence to certify uncertainty as fact. If the diagnosis was provisional, it should have been marked as provisional. If the feet were separate, their separation required explanation. If the pain was not Parkinsonian, then the Parkinson’s diagnosis did not answer the presenting complaint. If the pain was Parkinsonian, then the original foot-centred problem was neurologically central and should not have been displaced.

Every path leads back to the same conclusion: the foot problem retained diagnostic force.

This is why the defence has to fight its own documents. The documents say enough to support the patient’s reading. They say the case was atypical. They say the feet were present from the beginning. They say the gait was not ordinary. They say the footdrop-like movement was not foot drop. They say dystonia was in view. They say dopaminergic treatment was deferred. They say genetic possibility existed. They say pain later remained central. They say Parkinson’s medication affected the foot experience. They say pain was not directly Parkinson’s. They say possible dystonia could produce pain.

If the institution says the diagnosis was final, it must explain why its own record preserved so many reasons finality was unsafe. If it says the diagnosis was open, it must explain why it certified and relied on it as though it were final.

If the institution says the feet were separate, it must explain why they remained neurologically and dopaminergically entangled. If it says the feet were Parkinsonian, it must explain why the patient’s original protest was not decisive. If it says the pain was not Parkinsonian, it must explain why Parkinson’s medication was repeatedly used or considered to improve it.

This is not a case where the patient must overcome silence. It is a case where the patient can point to institutional speech.

The defence must not be allowed to hide inside the word review. Review of a patient is not review of a premise. Treatment adjustment is not diagnostic reopening. A medication change is not record correction. A follow-up appointment is not preserved provisionality. A symptom being discussed is not the same as a symptom being allowed to change the governing conclusion.

The patient’s position is sharper and more coherent: the documents show that the institution recorded an unresolved problem, certified through it, managed it for years, and later continued to describe it in internally unstable terms.

If the defence relies on the documents, the documents contain the contradiction. If it denies the contradiction, it fights the documents. If it says the contradiction was open, it must explain the certification. If it says the contradiction was closed, it must explain why the feet remained the central unresolved problem years later.

The record is not a defence exhibit.

It is the problem.
