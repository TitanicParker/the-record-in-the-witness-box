Entry signal

The Feet Were Not Incidental

On the GP referral, foot-centred entry, dystonic gait, and podiatry displacement.

The case entered through the feet. The record closed over them.

The feet were not incidental. That is the first fact that must be protected from dilution.

The case did not begin as an abstract diagnostic puzzle about Parkinson’s disease. It began as a failure of function expressed through the feet: abnormal gait, difficulty initiating movement, stiffness, increased tone, brisk reflexes, deteriorating occupational capacity, and severe bilateral foot pathology. The GP did not impose a diagnosis. He queried Parkinsonism or another neurological disorder, while preserving diagnostic openness.

The question was not simply whether this man had Parkinson’s disease. The question was why this man could not walk properly, and what relationship existed between the neurological signs and the condition of his feet.

Once the hospital accepted the referral, it accepted responsibility for that problem. It could diagnose Parkinsonism, but it still had to explain the feet. It could suspect a neurodegenerative syndrome, but it still had to decide whether the foot pathology was causal, secondary, dystonic, peripheral, compensatory, incidental, or some combination. It could move toward a working diagnosis, but it could not erase the problem that brought the patient into hospital.

The discharge summary contains the feet, but does not obey them. It records corns, calluses, pes planus, hammertoes, pain, abnormal gait, and a movement resembling foot drop but expressly not foot drop. It even gestures toward dystonic posture. But the feet do not become governing. They become background, conditional, perhaps separate, perhaps peripheral, perhaps suitable for podiatry.

This is not resolution. It is displacement.

A lawful and safe diagnostic sequence required one of three things: integrate the feet into the neurological diagnosis; falsify them as the governing signal; or explicitly supersede them with a tested explanation. The discharge summary does none of those things with adequate force.

Instead, it creates a grammatical hierarchy. Parkinson’s Disease is declarative. The feet are conditional. The abstraction receives final language. The body receives speculative language.

The podiatry referral is not harmless in context. Podiatry may have been appropriate as supportive care. The problem is that podiatry appears to function as an exit route for the unresolved signal. There is a crucial difference between support and displacement. Support says the patient has a neurological disorder and also needs foot care. Displacement moves the foot-centred problem outside neurological inquiry without explaining its neurological significance.

The footdrop-like description is especially important. If it looked like foot drop but was not true foot drop, what was it? If power was not the issue, why was the movement happening? The document’s own dystonic suggestion should have pulled the feet back into neurology, not pushed them outward.

Dystonia is not a decorative word in this case. A dystonic foot posture can produce abnormal loading, pain, callus formation, gait distortion, and apparent structural foot problems. If that possibility exists, surface pathology cannot safely be treated as the explanation. The foot may be the page on which the movement disorder writes itself.

The patient’s own objection was directed precisely at this point. Before discharge, he protested that the feet had not been addressed. He did not need technical vocabulary to be right. A patient can identify that a central problem remains unresolved before he knows the professional category into which it should fall.

The clean expert question is simple: where, before discharge and certification, did the institution resolve the foot-centred problem it accepted at referral?

If there is no answer, the diagnostic sequence did not complete.

The feet define the accepted scope of inquiry. They test the adequacy of the discharge summary. They expose the weakness of the podiatry displacement. They make the omission of protest material. They reveal the danger of finality.

The case entered through the feet.

The record closed over them.

That is the breach.
