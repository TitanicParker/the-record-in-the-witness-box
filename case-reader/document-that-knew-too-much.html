Discharge artefact

The Document That Knew Too Much

On the discharge summary as the central artefact: it records the red flags and then closes over them.

The document that was supposed to close the case instead discloses why the case was not closed.

The discharge summary is alarming not because it is empty, but because it is full. It does not simply miss the warning signs. It records youth, atypicality, abnormal gait, a footdrop-like movement expressly stated not to be foot drop, a posture suggestive of dystonia, severe foot pathology, minimal tremor, brisk reflexes, possible genetic disease, deliberate deferral of dopaminergic therapy, the possibility of later regret, and the author’s own statement that he is not a movement-disorders neurologist. Then, having recorded the reasons not to close, it closes.

That is the central fact.

A careless discharge summary would be easier to understand. It would omit complexity and leave only the blunt administrative diagnosis. This document does something more revealing. It preserves complexity while denying that complexity governing force. It makes uncertainty visible but non-operative. It allows atypicality into the narrative but not into the decision. It allows the feet into the history but not into the explanation. It allows levodopa into the treatment strategy but not into the diagnostic sequence. It allows subspecialty limitation into the prose but not into the pathway.

It is therefore not merely a discharge summary. It is a pressure document: a record trying to create finality out of facts that should have prevented finality.

At the top, the document lands. The primary diagnosis is Parkinson’s Disease. That line has administrative power. It is short, transferable, and usable. It can be copied into GP records. It can justify prescribing. It can support statutory certification. It can orient follow-up. It can become the premise of future clinical encounters.

But the body of the document does not read like a settled fact. It reads like an argument trying to persuade itself. The patient is young. The case is atypical. The tremor is minimal. The gait is strikingly abnormal. The foot movement resembles foot drop but is not foot drop. There is dystonic suggestion. There is severe foot pathology. There is preserved cognition. There is possible genetic disease. There is a decision to avoid levodopa. There is possible regret. There is the statement that the author is not a movement-disorders neurologist.

These are not decorative details. They are constraints. The document should have obeyed them.

Instead, it narrates them and proceeds past them.

The result is a document split against itself. Its administrative surface says certainty. Its clinical content says incompleteness. That split is the case.

The feet are the clearest example. They are everywhere in the document and nowhere in the governing conclusion. They appear in the history, the pain, the corns, calluses, hammertoes, pes planus, gait abnormality, and footdrop-like movement. Yet at closure they are not neurologically resolved. They are not integrated into the diagnosis. They are not shown to be incidental. They are conditionally separated and displaced toward podiatry.

That is not resolution. It is demotion.

A reader should pause over the grammar. Parkinson’s Disease is declarative. The feet are conditional. The diagnosis is stated. The initiating problem may be separate. The abstraction receives final language. The body receives provisional language. That is precisely backwards.

The levodopa issue exposes the same inversion. The document records a deliberate decision to avoid levodopa or dopamine agonist therapy because of youth and feared long-term consequences. That therapeutic justification can be defended as caution. But here levodopa was not only treatment. It was also a discriminator. It could have clarified the relationship between Parkinsonism, dystonic posture, gait, rigidity, pain, and dopaminergic responsiveness.

If levodopa was withheld, certainty had to be withheld too.

The phrase “we may regret that we did not start this earlier” is diagnostic foreboding. It admits foreseeable downside. Read in context, it means the team may later regret delaying the very intervention that could have clarified the case. That sentence should have prevented closure. Instead, it becomes part of the closure document.

The statement “I am not a movement-disorders neurologist” has the same structure. Here it is not harmless humility. It is jurisdictional. In a young, atypical, dystonic-looking, foot-centred, genetically possible, minimally tremulous case untreated with the principal discriminator, non-specialist status should trigger referral, supervision, or stricter provisionality. It should not sit passively inside the document that finalises the diagnosis.

A disclaimer without consequence is not a safeguard. It is insulation.

The document knew too much. That is its problem. It knew enough to qualify, but not enough to refrain. It knew enough to record red flags, but not enough to let them govern. It knew enough to produce a long and careful discharge summary, but not enough to prevent that summary from becoming the instrument of premature finality.

This is not a hindsight criticism. The warning signs are not imported from the future. They are on the page. The future did not create the uncertainty. The discharge summary preserved it.

The clinicians did not have to know the final genetic answer. They did not have to solve every complexity before discharge. They did have to preserve diagnostic status truthfully. They had to distinguish suspicion from certainty, treatment deferral from diagnostic completion, supportive podiatry from causal explanation, and humility about expertise from entitlement to finalise.

They did not.

The discharge summary contains both the prosecution and the defence. Its own richness defeats its own certainty. It saw too much. Then it closed anyway.
